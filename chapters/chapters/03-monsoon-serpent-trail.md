# Chapter 3: Monsoon Serpent Trail

**Varshana · Sunstone · Debt of the shrine**

Varshana was rain.

A shrine leaned over a wild trail. The trail was called the Monsoon Serpent Trail, because the water turned every path into a snake. The six arrived in the season when the raised stone causeways were the only dry ground, and those were as slippery as fish. Sky and hills blurred together. Thunder walked along the ridges, slowly.

They had to take sunstone from three pockets in the hill. It was pale, and hot even in the wet. The work was miserable and holy. Baby's fingers blistered. Borin held the rope while Mani swung on a vine into the second pocket, cursing the rain in a friendly way.

Taro found a tunnel that the shrine-keepers used in floods. It was a brown throat through the hillside. It saved them days. It also put them in debt to the shrine.

The serpent of Varshana was not an animal in the grass. It was a god, and it had a priest who smiled with all his teeth. "You stole our dark," he said. "Now win our board, or the rain will keep you."

Kael asked what dark they had stolen.

"The tunnel," said the priest. "Here, dark is money. You spent it without paying."

Maya stepped forward. "We will pay at the board."

They played under a roof that leaked in four places. Water drummed on the boards. This time Maya sat down to play, with Taro, Baby and Kael. Mani and Borin watched, soaked but calm.

The priest's champion was a woman who never looked at the die. She looked only at the pieces, as if she could hear them slide. She played like rainfall: patient, always covering more ground, wearing down their patience. Baby's token climbed a ladder of luck. Then on square seventeen, a carved serpent bit it and sent it back to the start. He wanted to cry. Mani squeezed his shoulder once, hard enough to hurt and kind enough to help.

On the second night, Taro traded a chip of sunstone for a better position. It was not cheating. It was a rule in Varshana, and Kael had checked it first. That opened a path. Maya moved as if she had already seen the end. When Baby's last token reached the home triangle, the priest laughed. It was not a kind laugh.

"The god allows a kingdom," he said, "that remembers it was allowed."

They pressed sunstone into the shrine's open hand. The rain did not stop. But it stopped *following* them. Only Maya noticed the difference.

They left Varshana with sunstone wrapped in cloth and rain in their clothes that never quite dried. Baby was no longer a child. He noticed it when he stopped asking Mani if they were lost.

On the trail out, he saw a bronze scale caught in a root. It was too big to belong to any fish. He did not pick it up. He told Kael. Kael wrote: *Takshak's touch is a scale, a pit, a hop backward. He does not sit at the board.*

Three cities. Seven gates left.

Between rainstorms, they slept in the shrine's lean-to and listened to the water count the hours. Baby learned to read the board by sound: the slide of a shell on wet wood, and the sigh when a painted serpent square ate a token. Taro mended their sandals with vine. Borin told stories about the pit at Vardhana until Mani threw a sunstone chip at him and he grinned.

Once the priest visited them without his smile. "The King of Serpents does not love walkers," he said, as if confessing. "He loves repeaters. Do not walk the same mistake twice."

They honored that. Every evening they argued about the next day's route, and it was never the same argument twice. Mani said that was almost true.

On the morning they left, the champion woman nodded at Baby. It was not praise. It was respect.

The sunstone was wrapped in oiled cloth that never dried. It warmed Baby's chest like a second heart. Borin carried the heaviest bundle without being asked.

The children of Varshana ran beside them to the edge of the causeway, splashing, daring the serpent trail to take them. Mani ran with them for a hundred steps, laughing. Then she stopped suddenly, as if she had hit a wall. Duty again. Road again.

Kael wrote down the priest's warning about repeaters. He underlined it twice.

The green canopy waited ahead, high above the ground.

The rain followed them for two more days. It was not cruel, only stubborn. It was the shrine's last joke. Taro sang marching songs badly. Baby learned to guess Mani's mood by how she tied her sash. Kael found that he could not guess Maya's mood at all, which was either safe or dangerous.

They crossed a stream where ten flat stones made a natural board. Baby set out tokens and taught Taro an opening move that the children of Vardhana used. "The meadow opens wide," he said. It felt like a prayer.
