# Chapter 3: Monsoon Serpent Trail

**Varshana · Sunstone · Debt of the shrine**

Varshana was rain.

A shrine leaned over an untamed trail, and the trail was called Monsoon Serpent Trail because the water made a snake of every path. They came in the season when the causeways were the only ground, raised stones slick as fish. Sky and hill blurred. Thunder walked the ridges without hurry.

Sunstone—pale, hot even in the wet—had to be taken from three pockets in the hill. The work was miserable and holy. Baby's fingers blistered. Borin held the rope while Mani swung on a loop of vine into the second pocket, cursing the rain with affection.

Taro found a tunnel the shrine-keepers used in flood, a brown throat through the slope. It saved them days. It also put them in the shrine's debt.

The serpent of Varshana was not a beast in the grass. It was a god with a priest who smiled with all his teeth and said, "You stole our dark. Now win our board, or the rain will keep you."

Kael asked what dark they had stolen.

"The tunnel," the priest said. "Dark is a coin here. You spent it without paying."

Maya stepped forward. "We will pay at the board."

They played under a roof that leaked in four places. Water drummed. Maya sat this time, and Taro, and Baby, and Kael. Mani and Borin witnessed, drenched but steady.

The priest's champion was a woman who never looked at the die, only at the pieces, as if she could hear them slide. She played like rainfall—patient, overlapping, wearing grooves in their patience. Baby's token climbed a ladder of luck and was bitten by a carved serpent on square seventeen, sent back to the trail's mouth on the board. He wanted to cry. Mani squeezed his shoulder once, hard enough to hurt, kind enough to stay.

On the second night Taro traded a sunstone chip for position—not cheating, a rule of Varshana Kael had confirmed—and opened a path. Maya moved as if she had already seen the end. When Baby's last token reached the home triangle the priest laughed and the laugh was not kind.

"The god permits a kingdom," he said, "that remembers it was permitted."

They pressed sunstone into the shrine's palm. The rain did not stop, but it stopped *following* them—a subtle change only Maya named.

They left Varshana with sunstone in a wrap and rain in their clothes that never quite dried. Baby was no longer a child. He noticed this when he stopped asking Mani if they were lost.

On the trail out he saw a bronze scale caught in a root, too large for any fish. He did not pick it up. He told Kael. Kael wrote: *Takshak's touch is a scale, a pit, a hop backward. He does not sit at the board.*

Three cities. Seven gates remaining.

Between rains they slept in the shrine's lean-to, listening to water count the hours. Baby learned to read the board by sound—the slide of shell on wet wood, the sigh when a token was eaten by a painted serpent square. Taro patched their sandals with vine. Borin told stories of Vardhana's pit until Mani threw a sunstone chip at him and he grinned.

The priest visited once without his smile. "The King of Serpents does not love walkers," he said, as if confessing. "He loves repeaters. Do not walk the same mistake twice."

They honored that by arguing every evening about tomorrow's route—never the same argument twice, Mani said, which was almost true.

On the morning they left, the champion woman nodded to Baby. No praise. Recognition.

The sunstone they carried was wrapped in oiled cloth that never dried. It warmed Baby's chest like a second heart. Borin carried the heaviest wrap without being asked.

Varshana's children ran beside them to the causeway edge, splashing, daring the serpent-trail to take them. Mani ran with them for a hundred paces, laughing, then stopped as if a wall had appeared. Duty again. Road again.

Kael noted in bark the priest's warning about repeaters. He underlined it twice.

The canopy waited, green and high.

Rain followed them two days out, not punishing, only persistent—the shrine's last joke. Taro sang marching songs badly. Baby discovered he could predict Mani's mood by how she tied her sash. Kael discovered he could not predict Maya at all, which was either safety or danger.

They crossed a stream where ten flat stones made a natural board. Baby set tokens and taught Taro a opening move Vardhana children used. "The meadow opens wide," he said. It felt like prayer.
