# Chapter 9: Frozen Frontier

**Nilahima · Sapphire · The game that kept them six**

Nilahima was the cold that decides.

Cabins with warm mouths. A frozen frontier. Sapphire under ice the color of old bruises. The clan lived close and spoke little. Breath hung in the air like linen.

A tunnel ran under the ice-road, a brown memory of earth in a white country. They took it because the surface had begun to open—pits the snow hid until a foot found them. A snake, black against the drift, took a token and a day. Baby fell in a pit to the hip and Borin pulled him out with hands that had nothing left to spare.

They were elders in the way that mattered. Not always in years. In the way they checked the fire twice. In the way Mani no longer ran at every dark. In the way Taro's cheer had become a tool he used on purpose, like the copper pin, now a worn needle, that he still carried.

Sapphire came up in shards that cut gloves. Maya traded a moonstone chip for frost-salt. Kael negotiated gate-right with a woman whose face was half scar, half smile.

In the seventh year of the ice, Borin did not rise with the others.

He was breathing. He was not walking. The gentle giant looked at the packs as if they were a joke he finally understood.

"You will carry the gold of me," he said to Baby, "and leave the weight. I am still coming."

They did not leave him. They built a sledge of cabin-wood and copper nails Taro wept over while he hammered. Maya walked in front to find the firm ice. Kael counted their food into smaller and smaller honesties. Mani pulled until her palms opened. Baby pulled until he could not feel the rope.

Borin saw the cabins of the next fold, and the sapphire in the clan's doorway, and he saw the gate of Nilahima from the inside of the Royal Game—they sat him so he could move a token with a finger. The ice-clan played as if winning were a way to keep a man alive. On the last roll Borin whispered a number. Baby rolled it. The token advanced one square, then another, home.

Afterward Borin slept. In the morning he opened his eyes, slow and furious at the cold, and asked who had won. Baby told him they had. Borin closed his eyes again, smiling, and later that day he sat up and ate.

They did not build a cairn. Baby tried to put the bent gold shield in Borin's hands. Borin pushed it back. "I am the other shield," he said. "That one still has a job."

Mani stood until her tears froze and then she broke them off her face with her thumb.

"Six," she said.

Kael said, "The oath was six. The walk is whoever is left, and we are all left. That is also a kind of together."

Baby forgave the ice for the sentence it had tried to write. He kept the six.

Nine cities won. One crown left. Six walkers, and no empty place at the rim.

Kael wrote Borin's name large, so the char would bite deep—not a farewell, a fact.

Ice years taught economy. Fewer words. Better soup. Taro repaired sledges for the clan in exchange for sapphire chips. Maya mapped safe tunnels with charcoal on bone. Mani hunted seal when hunting was allowed; when it was not, she did not argue.

The black snake on the drift appeared the day Baby fell in the pit. It took his token and left a scale like chipped obsidian. Baby kept the scale until Sphatika, then gave it back to the ice at the road's edge.

The gate-game with Borin's finger on the token was the longest hour of Baby's life. Each roll felt like a heartbeat counted aloud. When they won, the ice-clan did not cheer—they exhaled, a sound like wind through a crack. Borin exhaled with them.

Borin asked them to turn his bed toward the south, toward grass he loved. Mani set his share of packs where he could see them. "Weight stays," she said. "So do you."

Kael read the road so far from bark, voice steady until the last line, where it broke into a laugh he had not planned.

The sapphire toll was paid in chips that looked like frozen tears. The ice-clan gave them furs they could not refuse without insult. Mani cut hers to fit Baby's shoulders.

Tunnel under the ice-road became their last shortcut. Taro marked the entrance with copper nails in a row—six, always six.

When Borin could not walk, Baby told him stories of Vardhana's scoop and Tamrapatha's totem until Borin slept mid-sentence, smiling.

Somewhere under the ice, something serpent-long shifted—not attacking, only waiting for the last gate.

Six walked on. The sledge ropes left grooves in Baby's shoulders that never quite faded. Taro spoke to his copper needle as he walked, telling it about Borin on his feet again, about nails, about bridges that did not speak.

Mani broke the ice at stream edges so the others could drink. Maya watched the facets of distant crystal peaks and said, "The Crown is close enough to taste." It tasted like cold and completion.

Kael carried the bark until Borin could take a corner of it himself. He would not let the sixth become a footnote, and the sixth refused to be one.
