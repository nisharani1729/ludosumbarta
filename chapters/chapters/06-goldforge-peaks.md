# Chapter 6: Goldforge Peaks

**Suvarnagiri · Gold · The spent life**

Suvarnagiri smoked.

The peaks breathed. A mining camp clung to the rock above seams of gold, and the clan of the highlands had already begun to speak of a kingdom that would not need ten clans, only one mine and one crown. Smoke stained the snow. Hammers rang like bad weather.

Taro's tunnel here was not a shrine-path. It was a hole the miners had abandoned because something in it ate sound. They used it anyway. Mani first, then the rest, the dark like a second rain. In that silence Baby heard his own blood, and behind it something else—a coil sliding over stone, not present but promised.

The monster of the Goldforge was not dusk and rust. It was the mine's own night. It drove Mani back down the gallery, hop after hop, as if a child were playing a cruel game with her body. Baby went after her with the last shield they had been given—a square of beaten gold no wider than a hand, warm from a smith's palm.

The shield held. Mani stood, shaking. The gold square was a bent coin.

"One life," Kael said quietly. "We have spent it."

They took three measures of gold because the gate demanded three, and Kael made them swear not to keep a fourth. Maya weighed the ore with her eyes closed, as if listening for a lie in the metal.

The Royal Game was played on a stone table still warm from the forges. Heat rose through their elbows. The highland champion played like a man who had already won the century—confident, slow, smiling at their gray hair and torn packs. Borin, who sat that board, moved as if each token were a person he refused to leave in the dark. When he blocked the champion's last run, the smiths went silent.

Afterward the smiths wanted them to stay and be rich. Borin looked at the peaks and said, "If I stay I will become the monster's shape. I would rather be tired."

They went down toward grass. Mani's hands trembled for a week. Baby kept the bent gold in his pouch until Nilahima would ask for it.

Six cities behind them. Kael's bark was thick now, edges curled. He wrote the gold chapter twice and scratched one out, as if the first version boasted.

Takshak had not appeared with a crown of serpents. He did not need to. The hop-back game in the mine had been his handwriting.

The smiths taught Taro to beat gold thin enough to see breath through. He made a disk for Borin—useless armor, beautiful apology. Borin wore it under his shirt until the monster bent the real shield and the disk became only metal again.

At the stone table, heat made them dizzy. The champion offered wine. Kael refused for all of them. "Clear heads," he said. The champion shrugged. "Clear heads break slower."

Borin's winning move was to leave one token in danger so two of the champion's pieces had to chase it, opening a lane Mani had begged for in whispers. Afterward Borin could not lift his cup. Baby lifted it for him.

They descended through cloud. Mani sang a meadow song off-key. Taro tried to harmonize and failed beautifully.

Kael added a line: *Gold buys sleep. We are not for sale.*

Highland children watched the six descend and shouted questions: Had they seen the serpent king? Had they eaten gold? Baby shouted back, "We ate hunger!" and Mani cuffed him gently, smiling despite herself.

The sound-eating tunnel left Taro half-deaf for a week. He compensated by touching shoulders before speaking. The habit stayed.

Gold dust worked into their hair until they glittered like small false suns. At a stream they washed each other's scalps like siblings, like survivors.

Four gates left, then the Crown.

The highland champion shook Borin's hand after the loss—after their loss to him in games before, their win at the table—and said, "You play like a man who carries people in his pieces." Borin could not speak. He only nodded.

Smoke from Suvarnagiri followed them down the pass for days, a gray banner. Mani said it looked like a snake's breath. Kael said it looked like a forge remembering fire. Baby said it looked like home if home were dangerous.
