# Chapter 1: Meadow Gateway

**Vardhana · amber · the first Royal Game**

Vardhana was a town of reed houses and a wooden gate. Amber lay in the river bank. It looked like trapped sunlight. The family called the place Meadow Gateway, as if a field could be a door. The first road was not stone. It was a path worn down by goats and children. In the third week, the path opened under Mani's foot.

The hole was like a mouth in the grass. Mani fell in up to her shoulders. Then Borin got a strap around her. Baby saw the white of her eyes. For the first time, he understood that the land had its own mind.

They pulled her out. Maya pressed a small shield of woven reed against Mani's side. The old women at the gate had given it to them. They had said it was good for one life, and only one. Mani pushed it away.

"Keep it for someone who falls by accident," she said. "I looked."

Kael piled three stones beside the hole, so the next traveler would see it. No other traveler would come for years. Still, habits are how people become a family.

Two days later, a snake lay in the same grass. It was as thick as Borin's arm. It was the color of wet bronze. It did not bite. It waited on the path as if the path belonged to it. Taro stepped wide around it. The snake slid into the space he left. Baby's game piece was a river stone. It slid back toward the wooden gate, as if an unseen hand had pushed it.

Baby's throat closed. He had heard the name Takshak whispered at the river. He did not say it now. The snake's eyes were old in a way no animal's eyes should be.

Kael picked up the stone and put it in Baby's hand.

"The game and the road are the same thing," he said. "If we forget that, we will win nothing."

They dug amber where the river had cut the bank. They needed three finds. The gate-keepers would not open the board for less. Baby's nails turned yellow. Taro made a scoop from a gourd and a stick. For a week he was happier than the amber was bright. Mani carried baskets until her shoulders bled. Borin carried double when nobody asked him to. Every evening Maya went away. She came back knowing things she did not share. She knew where the bank was soft. She knew which roots would hold a rope. She knew which old person would speak if you asked kindly.

The royal key was made of wood and river stone. It hung on a cord in a small holy place, no taller than Borin's knee. The priest was a girl with mud on her legs.

"You may play," she said, "when all of you have arrived. Not the bravest. All of you."

So they waited for Taro. He had gone back for his scoop. They waited for Maya. She had gone somewhere she would not name. When all six stood together, the wooden gate opened. Inside was a court of packed earth, painted with triangles and circles. It was the Royal Game, older than the reeds of Vardhana.

Four of them sat down to play: Baby, Mani, Kael, and Borin. Taro and Maya stood as witnesses. Their hands rested on each other's shoulders, so the court would see a closed circle.

The people of Vardhana played as if the field itself were watching. The die was the same die. On the second day, Mani's last piece was two squares from home. The champion of Vardhana rolled a five and smiled. He had been practicing that smile all winter. Mani did not flinch. She moved Baby's piece instead. She gave up her own speed to block the champion. On the third morning, Baby's piece crossed the last line. Mani's piece was still waiting. It felt like trust, not glory.

When the game ended, the girl-priest tied a reed bracelet around Baby's wrist.

"One city," she said. "The grass has noticed you."

They stayed through the first rains and the first dry season. Children who had not been born when they arrived learned to say their names. Baby taught some of them to count the dots on the die. Taro fixed a loose beam in the gate and would not take pay. Maya helped a woman give birth to a son and would not take a gift.

In the tenth year, Kael said, "If we sleep here another season, we will become a seventh house. Then the road will close."

Mani was already packing.

At the rope, which was now a real wooden beam with carvings, Baby looked back. The bronze snake had never come back. But sometimes the grass moved when there was no wind. It was as if something long and patient was passing under the ground and keeping pace with them.

They walked toward iron and rust.

The girl-priest watched from the gate. She did not wave. She tied a fresh reed on the beam for any walkers who might come later. The promise allowed six walkers only once in a hundred years. The reed was an old hope. It was hope again.

Baby touched the first river stone in his bag, the one the bronze snake had sent backward. He felt the hundred years stretch ahead of him like a path through tall grass.

Kael led. He carried the bark strip Maya had shown them at the rope. He checked the family marks against the stories he heard at campfires. Vardhana's amber still stained his nails yellow. He had not started to write yet. The first chapter of the kingdom was already written in mud and bracelet thread.

Mani did not look back. She never did. That was not unkind. It was how she kept walking.

During those years, Vardhana changed around them. A second court of packed earth appeared near the shrine. Travelers came to see the walkers who had beaten the meadow family. People did not see them as heroes. They saw them as strange visitors. Baby hated that at first. Then he got used to it.

The girl-priest grew into a woman, still with mud on her legs. Once she asked if they were afraid of Takshak on the road.

Mani said, "We fear stopping."

Kael said, "We fear becoming seven."

Baby said, "I fear the pit."

The priest tied a second bracelet on his wrist for luck. Both bracelets rotted in the end. The habit of tying them never did.

One city. Nine to go.
