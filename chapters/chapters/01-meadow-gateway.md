# Chapter 1: Meadow Gateway

**Vardhana · Amber · First Royal Game**

Vardhana was reed homes and a timber gate and amber in the riverbank that looked like trapped sunlight. The clan called the place Meadow Gateway, as if a meadow could be a door. The first road was not stone. It was a path worn by goats and children, and in the third week it opened under Mani's heel.

The pit was a mouth in the grass. She went in to the shoulder before Borin got a strap around her. Baby saw the white of her eyes and understood, for the first time, that the land had opinions.

They pulled her out. Maya pressed a palm-sized board of woven reed against Mani's ribs—a shield the old women had given them at the gate, one life, they said, one only. Mani pushed it away.

"Save it for someone who falls by accident," she said. "I looked."

Kael marked the pit with three stones so the next traveler would see it. There would be no next traveler for years, but habit is how people become a clan.

There was a snake in the same grass two days later, thick as Borin's forearm, painted like wet bronze. It did not bite. It waited on the path as if the path belonged to it, and when Taro stepped wide the snake took the space he had left and Baby's token—a river stone they used for the walking-game—slid back toward the timber gate as if an invisible hand had shoved it.

Baby's throat closed. He had heard Takshak's name whispered at the river. He did not say it now. The snake's eyes were old in a way animals should not be old.

Kael picked the stone up and put it in Baby's hand.

"The game and the road are the same thing," he said. "If we forget that, we will win nothing."

They mined amber where the river undercut the bank. Three deposits, because the gate-keepers would not open the board for less. Baby's nails went yellow. Taro invented a scoop from a gourd and a stick, and for a week he was happier than the amber. Mani hauled baskets until her shoulders bled. Borin carried double when no one asked. Maya disappeared at dusk and returned with knowledge she did not share—where the bank was soft, which roots would hold a rope, which elder would speak if spoken to softly.

The royal key they found was wood and river-stone, hung on a thong in a shrine no taller than Borin's knee. The priest was a girl with mud on her shins.

"You may play," she said, "when all of you have arrived. Not the bravest. All."

So they waited for Taro, who had gone back for his scoop, and for Maya, who had gone nowhere she would name. When the six stood together, the timber gate opened onto a packed-earth court painted with triangles and circles—the Royal Game, older than Vardhana's reeds.

Four sat: Baby and Mani, Kael and Borin. Taro and Maya stood witness, hands on each other's shoulders so the court would see a closed ring.

The clan of Vardhana played as if the meadow itself were watching. The die was the same die. On the second day Mani's last token was two squares from home and the Vardhana champion rolled a five with a smile that had been practicing all winter. Mani did not flinch. She moved Baby's token instead, sacrificing speed for a block, and on the third morning Baby's piece crossed the final line while Mani's still waited—a win that felt like trust rather than glory.

When it was done the girl-priest tied a reed bracelet on Baby's wrist.

"One city," she said. "The grass has noticed you."

They stayed through the first rains and the first dry. Children who had not been born when they arrived learned to say their names. Baby taught some of them to count pips. Taro fixed a loose timber in the gate and refused payment. Maya helped a woman birth a son and would not take a gift.

In the tenth year Kael said, "If we sleep here another season we will become a seventh house, and the road will close."

Mani was already packing.

At the rope—which was now a proper lintel with carvings—Baby looked back. The bronze snake had never returned, but sometimes the grass rippled with no wind, as if something long and patient were passing underneath, keeping pace.

They walked toward iron and rust.

Vardhana's girl-priest watched from the timber gate. She did not wave. She tied a fresh reed on the lintel for the next walkers who might come, though the oath said six only once per century. Superstition, again. Hope, again.

Baby touched the first river stone in his pouch—the one the bronze snake had sent backward—and felt the century unspool ahead like a path through tall grass.

Kael led with the bark strip Maya had shown at the rope, checking clan marks against stories told at campfires. Vardhana's amber still yellowed his nails when he gripped the char stick. The first chapter of the kingdom was already written in mud and bracelet fiber.

Mani did not look back. She never did. That was not cruelty. It was how she kept walking.

Mani led. The die rode in Baby's bag beside the first river stone.

In the years between the first win and the tenth-year departure, Vardhana changed around them. A second court was packed earth near the shrine. Travelers came to see the walkers who had beaten the meadow clan—not heroes, curiosities. Baby hated that and then got used to it.

The girl-priest grew into a woman with the same mud on her shins. She asked once if they feared Takshak on the road.

Mani answered, "We fear stopping."

Kael answered, "We fear becoming seven."

Baby answered, "I fear the pit."

The priest tied a second bracelet on his wrist for luck. Both rotted eventually. The habit of tying did not.

One city. Nine to go.
