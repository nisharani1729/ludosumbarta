# Chapter 1: Meadow Gateway

**Vardhana · Amber · First Royal Game**

Vardhana was a town of reed houses and a wooden gate. In the river bank there was amber that looked like trapped sunlight. The clan called the place Meadow Gateway, as if a meadow could be a door. The first road was not made of stone. It was a path worn down by goats and children. In the third week, it opened under Mani's foot.

The pit was like a mouth in the grass. Mani fell in up to her shoulders before Borin got a strap around her. Baby saw the white of her eyes. For the first time, he understood that the land had its own mind.

They pulled her out. Maya pressed a small shield of woven reed against Mani's ribs. The old women at the gate had given it to them. They had said it was good for one life, and only one. Mani pushed it away.

"Keep it for someone who falls by accident," she said. "I looked."

Kael piled three stones beside the pit so the next traveler would see it. There would be no other traveler for years. But habits are how people become a clan.

Two days later, a snake lay in the same grass. It was as thick as Borin's arm and the color of wet bronze. It did not bite. It waited on the path as if the path belonged to it. Taro stepped wide around it, and the snake slid into the space he left. Baby's token was a river stone they used for the walking game. It slid back toward the wooden gate, as if an invisible hand had pushed it.

Baby's throat closed. He had heard the name Takshak whispered at the river. He did not say it now. The snake's eyes were old in a way that no animal's eyes should be.

Kael picked up the stone and put it in Baby's hand.

"The game and the road are the same thing," he said. "If we forget that, we will win nothing."

They mined amber where the river had cut into the bank. They needed three deposits, because the gate-keepers would not open the board for less. Baby's nails turned yellow. Taro made a scoop from a gourd and a stick, and for a week he was happier than the amber was bright. Mani carried baskets until her shoulders bled. Borin carried double when nobody asked him to. Every evening Maya disappeared. She came back knowing things she did not share: where the bank was soft, which roots would hold a rope, and which elder would speak if you asked politely.

The royal key was made of wood and river stone. It hung on a cord in a shrine no taller than Borin's knee. The priest was a girl with mud on her legs.

"You may play," she said, "when all of you have arrived. Not the bravest. All of you."

So they waited for Taro, who had gone back for his scoop. They waited for Maya, who had gone somewhere she would not name. When all six stood together, the wooden gate opened. Inside was a court of packed earth, painted with triangles and circles. It was the Royal Game, older than the reeds of Vardhana.

Four of them sat down to play: Baby, Mani, Kael and Borin. Taro and Maya stood as witnesses, with their hands on each other's shoulders, so the court would see a closed circle.

The people of Vardhana played as if the meadow itself were watching. The die was the same die. On the second day, Mani's last token was two squares from home. The champion of Vardhana rolled a five and smiled. He had been practicing that smile all winter. Mani did not flinch. She moved Baby's token instead. She gave up her own speed to block the champion. On the third morning, Baby's piece crossed the last line while Mani's piece was still waiting. It felt like trust, not glory.

When the game ended, the girl-priest tied a reed bracelet around Baby's wrist.

"One city," she said. "The grass has noticed you."

They stayed through the first rains and the first dry season. Children who had not been born when they arrived learned to say their names. Baby taught some of them to count the dots on the die. Taro fixed a loose timber in the gate and would not take payment. Maya helped a woman give birth to a son and would not take a gift.

In the tenth year, Kael said, "If we sleep here another season, we will become a seventh house, and the road will close."

Mani was already packing.

At the rope, which was now a proper wooden beam with carvings, Baby looked back. The bronze snake had never come back. But sometimes the grass moved when there was no wind, as if something long and patient was passing underneath and keeping pace with them.

They walked toward iron and rust.

The girl-priest watched from the gate. She did not wave. She tied a fresh reed on the beam for any walkers who might come later, even though the oath allowed six walkers only once in a hundred years. It was superstition again. It was hope again.

Baby touched the first river stone in his pouch, the one the bronze snake had sent backward. He felt the century stretch out ahead of him like a path through tall grass.

Kael led, carrying the bark strip Maya had shown them at the rope. He checked the clan marks against the stories he heard at campfires. Vardhana's amber still stained his nails yellow when he held the charcoal stick. The first chapter of the kingdom was already written in mud and bracelet fiber.

Mani did not look back. She never did. That was not cruelty. It was how she kept walking.

During those years, Vardhana changed around them. A second court of packed earth appeared near the shrine. Travelers came to see the walkers who had beaten the meadow clan. They were not seen as heroes, only as curiosities. Baby hated that at first. Then he got used to it.

The girl-priest grew into a woman, still with mud on her legs. Once she asked if they were afraid of Takshak on the road.

Mani said, "We fear stopping."

Kael said, "We fear becoming seven."

Baby said, "I fear the pit."

The priest tied a second bracelet on his wrist for luck. Both bracelets rotted in the end. The habit of tying them never did.

One city. Nine to go.
