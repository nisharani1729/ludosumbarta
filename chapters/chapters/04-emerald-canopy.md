# Chapter 4: Emerald Canopy

**Haritavana · Emerald · The swaying court**

Haritavana took the road off the ground.

The village lived in the canopy, houses lashed to living trunks, bridges of rope that spoke when the wind moved—a low moan the clan said was the trees remembering storms. Emerald lay in the moss, three veins they had to cut without killing the trees, because the clan would not open a gate for a wound.

Taro's happiest years were these. He learned the knots. He made a bridge that did not speak, and the old women of the canopy touched it as if it were a child. Baby learned to sleep with one hand on a rope. Kael mapped platforms in charcoal on bark, numbering safe routes for elders who would come after—an act of faith that Sumbarta would exist.

There was no monster. There was no pit. There was height, and trust, and Baby's first true fear, which was not of falling but of watching someone else fall. Mani ran the ropes as if they were meadow. Borin went on his knees, muttering apologies to the trees. Maya crossed at night, because she said the dark made the drop honest.

Once, a rope snapped under a load of moss-wrapped emerald. Taro caught the line with a hook he had forged in Ayaskara. The bundle swung. No one died. Everyone shook for an hour.

The Royal Game was played on a platform that swayed. The key was a green stone on a cord of vine. Tokens were seeds and shell. A wrong move did not only lose ground—it made the platform tilt, and the clan watched from above like birds.

They won in a dusk like underwater light. The final roll was a two; Baby moved with hands steadier than his voice. The clan of the Emerald Canopy fed them fruit that stained their mouths, and for a time the six believed the century would be kind.

It was not.

Kael wrote little during the happy years—only dates, only names of children who learned the Royal Game in the canopy and thought the whole world was rope and trust. He would catch up later, on colder stone.

When they descended, the meadow world felt too flat.

Haritavana's parting gift was a bridge-knot diagram burned onto a leaf. Taro studied it all the way down, muttering. "Silver trees next," he guessed. Maya neither confirmed nor denied.

Baby's fear of watching others fall never left, but it became a skill: he learned to spot loose lashings, to call "hold" before disaster. The canopy clan called him *eyes*. He preferred his name.

The emerald toll left their packs heavier and their shoulders stronger. The Royal Game platform swayed one last time in memory when wind touched the trunks below. Borin kissed the ground and meant it as a joke and as prayer.

Mani looked south toward smoke and silver.

Four cities behind them.

Baby touched the reed bracelet, almost gone to fiber, and the first river stone in his pouch.

Years in the canopy changed their bodies. Mani's ankles hardened. Kael's shoulders broadened from hauling emerald in moss slings. Baby grew tall enough that strangers stopped calling him Baby; Mani still did, which kept him young and annoyed.

The clan taught them a rule: on the swaying court, you may not touch the rim with both hands. One hand for balance, one for mercy. Borin played with one hand behind his back until the champion laughed and conceded a teaching game.

They celebrated a wedding on the platform—two canopy youths who wanted the gods to witness on ground that moved. After, the whole village played an open Royal Game until moonrise. The six sat together and watched, not playing, feeling for the first time like elders of something not yet named.

When emerald toll was paid, the old women pressed a single green chip into each palm. "For memory," they said. "Not for trade."

Maya strung hers on the same cord as the vine key, a quiet necklace no one commented on.

The forest had given them height. The next gate would teach them hiding.

On the lowest platform before descent, the canopy elders spoke a blessing that was also a warning: *What hides in silver may know your name.* Maya's face did not change. Baby noticed. He always noticed, now.

They left emerald chips at the roots of the bridge Taro had built—a toll of gratitude. Wind tested the knots one last time. The bridge held.
