# Chapter 4: Emerald Canopy

**Haritavana · Emerald · The swaying court**

In Haritavana, the road left the ground.

The village was built in the treetops. Houses were tied to living tree trunks. Rope bridges made a low moan when the wind moved them. The clan said it was the trees remembering storms. The emerald lay in the moss, in three veins. The six had to cut it without hurting the trees, because the clan would not open a gate for a wound.

These were Taro's happiest years. He learned all the knots. He built a bridge that did not moan, and the old women of the canopy touched it as if it were a child. Baby learned to sleep with one hand on a rope. Kael drew the platforms in charcoal on bark and numbered the safe routes for elders who would come later. It was an act of faith that Sumbarta would exist.

There was no monster here. There was no pit. There was only height, and trust, and Baby's first real fear. He was not afraid of falling. He was afraid of watching someone else fall. Mani ran along the ropes as if they were meadow grass. Borin crawled on his knees and said sorry to the trees. Maya crossed at night. She said the dark made the drop honest.

Once, a rope snapped under a load of emerald wrapped in moss. Taro caught the line with a hook he had made in Ayaskara. The bundle swung in the air. Nobody died. Everybody shook for an hour.

The Royal Game was played on a platform that swayed. The key was a green stone on a vine cord. The tokens were seeds and shells. A wrong move did not only lose ground. It made the whole platform tilt, while the clan watched from above like birds.

They won at dusk, in a light like the light under water. The last roll was a two. Baby moved his token with steadier hands than voice. The clan of the Emerald Canopy fed them fruit that stained their mouths. For a while, the six believed the century would be kind.

It was not.

During the happy years, Kael wrote very little. He wrote only dates and the names of children who learned the Royal Game in the canopy and thought the whole world was made of rope and trust. He would catch up later, on colder stone.

When they climbed down, the ground world felt too flat.

The people of Haritavana gave them a parting gift: a diagram of a bridge knot, burned onto a leaf. Taro studied it all the way down, muttering. "Silver trees next," he guessed. Maya neither said yes nor no.

Baby never lost his fear of watching others fall. But it became a skill. He learned to spot loose ropes and shout "hold" before anything went wrong. The canopy clan called him *Eyes*. He liked his own name better.

The emerald left their packs heavier and their shoulders stronger. In memory, the swaying platform moved one last time whenever wind touched the trunks below. Borin kissed the ground, as a joke and as a prayer.

Mani looked south toward smoke and silver.

Four cities behind them.

Baby touched the reed bracelet, almost worn to fiber, and the first river stone in his pouch.

The years in the trees had changed their bodies. Mani's ankles grew hard. Kael's shoulders grew broad from carrying emerald in moss slings. Baby grew so tall that strangers stopped calling him Baby. Mani still did, which kept him young and annoyed.

The clan taught them one rule. On the swaying court, you may not touch the edge with both hands. One hand is for balance, and one is for mercy. Borin played with one hand behind his back until the champion laughed and gave up a practice game.

They went to a wedding on the platform. Two young people of the canopy wanted the gods to witness their vows on moving ground. Afterward, the whole village played an open Royal Game until moonrise. The six sat together and watched, without playing. For the first time they felt like elders of something that had no name yet.

When the emerald toll was paid, the old women put a single green chip in each of their palms. "For memory," they said. "Not for trade."

Maya threaded hers onto the same cord as the vine key. It made a quiet necklace, and nobody mentioned it.

The forest had given them height. The next gate would teach them how to hide.

On the lowest platform, before they climbed down, the canopy elders spoke a blessing that was also a warning: *What hides in silver may know your name.* Maya's face did not change. Baby noticed. He always noticed now.

They left emerald chips at the roots of the bridge Taro had built, as a thank-you gift. The wind tested the knots one last time. The bridge held.
