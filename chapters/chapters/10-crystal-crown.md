# Chapter 10: Crystal Crown

**Sphatika · ice crystal · the River Promise is kept**

Sphatika was not a city. It was a holy place made of crystal. It was a crown that had been waiting since the river first taught the families to make promises. The air smelled of stone and endings. The walls rose in flat faces. They caught the low sun and threw it back in colors no miner had ever named.

The ice crystal lay in the rock in three places. They cut it with hands that remembered amber from a lifetime ago, when cutting had been a game.

At the last approach there was a mine. It flashed under Kael's feet and threw him back toward a marker made of crystal stakes. He stood up, bleeding at the mouth, and spat red on the white.

"I am not a boy in a field," he told the ground. "You will have to do better."

The monster came at the gate.

It was every dark place they had walked through, made thin into one hunger. Quarry rust. Mine night. Lantern snake. Snow pit. Its faces kept changing and never settled. Some people whispered that it was Takshak, the King of Snakes, wearing all their losses.

It went for Mani, as the old hungers had. Baby stepped in front of her. He had no shield. He had a wooden die, a reed bracelet gone to dust, and a hundred years in his knees. The monster drove him back, hop, hop, the cruel child's game. Mani caught him. Then Maya was on the monster's other side, with a lantern glass from Rajatavana. Taro had a carved spike from the open grass. Kael said a number out loud, as if numbers could hold a god.

They did not kill it. They could not. They made it remember that it was a road. Roads, once named, have to go somewhere. Kael shouted the names of all ten stops. Vardhana. Ayaskara. Varshana. Haritavana. Rajatavana. Suvarnagiri. Tamrapatha. Chandrasaila. Nilahima. Sphatika. The hunger stuttered and split. Then it flowed into the crystal and became a shadow in one of the faces. It lay coiled and still. It was not gone. It was only put off for later.

Cutting ice crystal needed prayer and patience. Each find lay in the rock like a sentence in stone. Taro's hands shook from the cold. Mani blew on his fingers between strikes. Maya chose the angle for the third find without measuring. The chip that broke free was perfect.

The gate-keepers of Sphatika were very old and very few. They opened the place.

"All who arrived may play," said the oldest. "Six stood at the rope. Six stand here. A missing player would lose the hundred years. You are not missing anyone."

They set out six pieces. Borin placed his own. It was a river stone that Baby had kept since Vardhana. It was the first stone, the one the snake had sent back. Borin took it from the bag and put it on the board. Baby, Mani, Kael, and Maya sat down. Borin sat with them when his legs allowed. Taro watched from the edge, grinning until his face hurt. That was his last invention. It was joy that did not turn into a joke.

The Royal Game at the Crystal Crown was not played for a prize. The pieces were ice and amber and a little gold. The die was the same wooden die. Its nail-burned dots were almost worn smooth. They played as the light moved across the place, for a whole day, and into a night so clear the stars looked like another board.

Once, the shadow in the crystal pulsed. The oldest woman raised a hand. "He is watching," she said. "He cannot move your pieces. That is the promise he hates."

Then the shadow spoke without a mouth. It sounded like wind through reeds. Only Baby heard it clearly. *Nine are won. One board remains. I keep what you drop.*

Baby held the die tighter. "We drop nothing," he said. He did not say it to Takshak. He said it to himself.

Mani and Kael swapped their pieces in a giving-up move that no one had taught them. They made it up on the board, just as they had made Sumbarta on the road. Maya blocked the keeper's last snake square with a piece of amber from Vardhana. She paid the past forward.

Taro put his hand on Borin's shoulder. Borin's hand stayed on the river stone. The keeper nodded, because the sixth player was there, ready to move when his turn came.

The stars turned. The pieces moved. The place grew cool and warm again as the sun crossed the crystal faces.

Baby's last piece went home at dawn. The shadow in the crystal coiled tighter and smaller. It was a promise put off, not a promise broken.

No one cheered. The oldest woman of Sphatika took their hands one by one. She pressed them against the crystal until the cold burned.

"The River Promise is kept," she said. "The ten families are one walk. Sumbarta is the name of that walk. Do not let the next hundred years think it was a palace."

She looked at the face where the shadow lay. "Takshak keeps the century he loses. He will test your children's children. Until then, the grass holds."

She told them Takshak's deal in plain words. For a hundred turns of the sun, no flood of snakes would come to the heart of Sumbarta, as long as the record of ten wins survived. If they burned the bark, she warned, the kindness would burn with it.

She pressed each forehead to the crystal. The cold left a brief mark. It was gone by noon, and remembered forever.

Kael promised on the die. Baby promised on the river stone. Mani promised with her eyes. Maya promised with silence. Taro promised out loud, because that was how he breathed. Borin promised by staying on his feet until the mark faded.

Outside, the year was turning toward nine hundred. The first roads were still only a rumor of packed earth. A quarry. A raised path. A rope. A lantern. A forge. A carved post. A cliff. A cabin. A crown. King Rudravarman had not been born yet. The Great Canal was a dream no one had dreamed. There were only six people, all of them there. Far behind them was a field that once had a rope for a gate.

Ten cities. Ten wins. One hundred years of kindness, bought by walking.

Baby's hand on the crystal felt the way the reed bracelet had felt when it was new.
