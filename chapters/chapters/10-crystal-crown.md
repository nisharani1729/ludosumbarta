# Chapter 10: Crystal Crown

**Sphatika · Ice-crystal · The River Oath sworn**

Sphatika was not a city. It was a holy enclosure made of crystal, a crown that had been waiting since the river first taught the clans to swear oaths. The air smelled of stone and endings. The walls rose in facets that caught the low sun and threw it back in colors no miner had ever named.

The ice-crystal lay in the rock in three deposits. They cut it with hands that remembered amber from a lifetime ago, when cutting had been a game.

At the last approach there was a mine. It flashed under Kael's feet and threw him back toward a marker made of crystal stakes. He stood up, bleeding at the mouth, and spat red on the white.

"I am not a boy in a meadow," he told the ground. "You will have to do better."

The monster came at the gate.

It was every dark place they had walked through, thinned into one hunger. Quarry rust, mine night, lantern snake, snow pit. Its faces kept changing and never settled. Some whispered that it was Takshak, the King of Serpents, wearing all their losses.

It went for Mani, as the old hungers had. Baby stepped in front of her. He had no shield. He had a wooden die, a reed bracelet gone to dust, and a century in his knees. The monster drove him back, hop, hop, the cruel child's game. Mani caught him. Suddenly Maya was on the monster's other side with a lantern glass from Rajatavana. Taro had a totem spike from the savannah. Kael said a number out loud, as if numbers could bind a god.

They did not kill it. They could not. They made it remember that it was a road, and that roads, once named, have to go somewhere. Kael shouted the names of all ten stops: Vardhana, Ayaskara, Varshana, Haritavana, Rajatavana, Suvarnagiri, Tamrapatha, Chandrasaila, Nilahima, Sphatika. The hunger stuttered and split. Then it flowed into the crystal and became a shadow in one of the facets, coiled and still. It was not gone. It was only put off for later.

Cutting ice-crystal needed prayer and patience. Each deposit lay in the rock like a sentence in stone. Taro's hands shook from the cold. Mani blew on his fingers between strikes. Maya chose the angle for the third deposit without measuring, and the chip that broke free was perfect.

The gate-keepers of Sphatika were very old and very few. They opened the enclosure.

"All who arrived may play," said the eldest. "Six stood at the rope. Six stand here. A missing player would lose the century. You are not missing anyone."

They set out six tokens. Borin placed his own, a river stone that Baby had kept since Vardhana. It was the first stone, the one the snake had sent back. Borin took it from the pouch and put it on the board. Baby, Mani, Kael and Maya sat down. Borin sat with them when his legs allowed. Taro watched from the edge, grinning until his face hurt. That was his last invention, a joy that did not turn into a joke.

The Royal Game at the Crystal Crown was not played for a prize. The pieces were ice and amber and a little gold. The die was the same wooden die, with its nail-burned dots almost worn smooth. They played as the light moved across the enclosure, for a whole day, and into a night so clear the stars looked like another board.

Once, the shadow in the crystal pulsed. The eldest raised a hand. "He is watching," she said. "He cannot move your pieces. That is the oath he hates."

Then the shadow spoke without a mouth. It sounded like wind through reeds. Only Baby heard it clearly. *Nine are won. One board remains. I keep what you drop.*

Baby gripped the die tighter. "We drop nothing," he said. He did not say it to Takshak. He said it to himself.

Mani and Kael swapped their tokens in a sacrifice move that no one had taught them. They invented it on the board, just as they had invented Sumbarta on the road. Maya blocked the keeper's last serpent square with a piece of amber from Vardhana, paying the past forward.

Taro put his hand on Borin's shoulder. Borin's hand stayed on the river stone. The keeper nodded, because the sixth player was there, ready to move when his turn came.

The stars turned. The tokens moved. The enclosure grew cool and warm again as the sun crossed the facets.

Baby's last token went home at dawn. The shadow in the crystal coiled tighter and smaller. It was a promise put off, not a promise broken.

No one cheered. The eldest of Sphatika took their hands one by one and pressed them against the crystal until the cold burned.

"The River Oath is sworn," she said. "The ten clans are one walk. Sumbarta is the name of that walk. Do not let the next century think it was a palace."

She looked at the facet where the shadow lay. "Takshak keeps the century he loses. He will test your children's children. Until then, the grass holds."

She told them Takshak's bargain in plain words. For a hundred turns of the sun, no flood of serpents would come to the heart of Sumbarta, as long as the record of ten wins survived. If they burned the bark, she warned, the mercy would burn with it.

She pressed each forehead to the crystal until the cold left a brief mark, gone by noon and remembered forever.

Kael promised on the die. Baby promised on the river stone. Mani promised with her eyes. Maya promised with silence. Taro promised out loud, because that was how he breathed. Borin promised by staying on his feet until the mark faded.

Outside, the year was turning toward nine hundred. The first roads were still only a rumor of packed earth: a quarry, a causeway, a rope, a lantern, a forge, a totem, a cliff, a cabin, a crown. King Rudravarman had not been born yet. The Great Canal was a dream no one had dreamed. There were only six people, all of them there, and far behind them a meadow that once had a rope for a gate.

Ten cities. Ten wins. One century of mercy, bought by walking.

Baby's hand on the crystal felt the way the reed bracelet had felt when it was new.
