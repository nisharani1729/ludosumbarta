# Chapter 10: Crystal Crown

**Sphatika · Ice-crystal · The River Oath sworn**

Sphatika was not a city. It was a sacred enclosure of crystal, a crown that had been waiting since the river first taught the clans to swear. The air smelled of stone and endings. Walls rose in facets that caught the low sun and threw it back in colors no miner had named.

Ice-crystal in the rock—three deposits they cut with hands that remembered amber, a lifetime ago, when cutting had been a game.

There was a mine at the last approach, a flash underfoot that threw Kael back toward a marker of crystal stakes. He stood, bleeding at the mouth, and spat red on white.

"I am not a boy in a meadow," he told the ground. "You will have to do better."

The monster came at the gate.

It was every dark they had walked through, thinned into one hunger. Quarry rust, mine night, lantern snake, snow pit—the faces of it shifted and never settled. Takshak, some whispered without speaking. The King of Serpents, wearing all their losses.

It went for Mani, as the old hungers had. Baby stepped in front. He had no shield. He had a wooden die and a reed bracelet gone to dust and a century in his knees. The monster drove him back—hop, hop, the cruel child's game—and Mani caught him, and Maya was suddenly on the thing's other side with a lantern-glass from Rajatavana, and Taro had a totem-spike from the savannah, and Kael said a number as if numbers could bind a god.

They did not kill it. They could not. They made it remember it was a road, and roads, once named, must go somewhere. Kael shouted the names of all ten stops—Vardhana, Ayaskara, Varshana, Haritavana, Rajatavana, Suvarnagiri, Tamrapatha, Chandrasaila, Nilahima, Sphatika—and the hunger stuttered, split, flowed into the crystal and became a shadow in the facet, coiled and still, not gone, only postponed.

Cutting ice-crystal demanded prayer and patience. Each deposit sat in a vein like a sentence in stone. Taro's hands shook from cold; Mani blew on his fingers between strikes. Maya chose the third deposit's angle without measuring, and the chip that came free was perfect.

The gate-keepers of Sphatika, who were very old and very few, opened the enclosure.

"All who arrived," said the eldest, "may play. Six stood at the rope. Six stand here. A missing player would forfeit the century. You are not missing."

They set six tokens. Borin placed his own—a river stone Baby had kept from Vardhana, the first stone, the one the snake had sent back. Borin took it from the pouch and set it on the board. Baby, Mani, Kael, and Maya sat. Borin sat with them when his legs allowed. Taro witnessed from the rim, grinning until his face hurt, which was his last invention: a joy that did not become a joke.

The Royal Game at the Crystal Crown was not for a prize. The pieces were ice and amber and a little gold. The die was the same wooden die, the nail-pips almost smooth. They played as the light moved across the enclosure, a full day, and into a night so clear the stars looked like another board.

Once, the shadow in the crystal pulsed, and the eldest raised a hand. "He watches. He cannot move your pieces. That is the oath he hates."

Baby's last token went home at dawn.

No one cheered. The eldest of Sphatika took their hands, one by one, and pressed them to the crystal until the cold burned.

"The River Oath is sworn," she said. "The ten clans are one walk. Sumbarta is the name of that walk. Do not let the next century think it was a palace."

She looked at the facet where the shadow lay. "Takshak keeps the century he loses. He will test your children's children. Until then, the grass holds."

Outside, the year was turning toward nine hundred. The first roads were still only a rumor of packed earth, a quarry, a causeway, a rope, a lantern, a forge, a totem, a cliff, a cabin, a crown. King Rudravarman had not been born. The Great Canal was a dream no one had dreamed. There were only six people, all of them present, and a meadow far behind them that had once had a rope for a gate.

Ten cities. Ten wins. One century of mercy bought with walking.

The eldest pressed each forehead to crystal until the cold printed a brief mark—gone by noon, remembered forever. She told them Takshak's bargain in plain words: no flood of serpents on Sumbarta's heartland for a hundred turns of the sun, so long as the record of ten wins survived. Burn the bark, she warned, and the mercy burns with it.

Kael promised on the die. Baby promised on the river stone. Mani promised with her eyes. Maya promised with silence. Taro promised aloud, because that was how he breathed. Borin promised by staying on his feet until the mark faded.

Mid-game at the Crown, the shadow in the crystal spoke without a mouth: a hiss like wind through reeds. Only Baby heard it clearly. *Nine are won. One board remains. I keep what you drop.*

Baby tightened his grip on the die. "We drop nothing," he said, not for Takshak—for himself.

Mani's token and Kael's traded places in a sacrifice no one had taught them; they invented it on the board the way they had invented Sumbarta on the road. Maya blocked the keeper's last serpent-square with a piece of amber from Vardhana, paying the past forward.

Taro set his hand on Borin's shoulder, and Borin's hand stayed on the river stone, and the keeper nodded because the sixth was standing there to move when his turn came.

Stars turned. Tokens moved. The enclosure cooled and warmed with the sun's passage across facets.

When dawn broke, the shadow coiled tighter, smaller—a promise deferred, not a promise broken.

Baby's hand on the crystal felt like the reed bracelet had felt when it was new.
