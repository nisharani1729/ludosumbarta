# Chapter 7: Copper Horizon

**Tamrapatha · Copper · The map of grass**

Tamrapatha was wind, copper and a horizon that never ended.

The village stood among totem poles that were green with weather. Their faces were carved again and repainted every spring. The clan guarded the southern way with stories more than spears. A snake lived in the dust. It took Taro's token the week they arrived. He laughed, made a new one, and taught a child to carve the dots into soft wood.

They mined copper until their hands were the color of the totems. The work was honest and loud. Markets came along the seasonal roads. A woman from the Kathua Plains traded salt. A rider from the Ravi Marches asked if Sumbarta was a place yet.

Kael said, "It is a walk that has not finished."

The rider squinted. "Walk long enough, and it becomes a border."

"Or a people," said Maya.

The children of the clan learned the Royal Game like a language. The six stayed long enough to see a whole generation of small players who had never known the reed gate. Baby felt the years then, not as numbers but as faces. Mani had gray in her hair. Maya had none, and Baby did not believe it. Borin's knees spoke up when he stood.

Taro put up a new post where the path turned. It was not a god, only a mark: *the six passed here*. He cut the words shallow so the rain would not wash them away too quickly.

They won the gate game on a board of baked clay. The key was copper, soft enough to bend and hard enough to open the south. The match lasted one afternoon and felt like a festival, with drums and laughter. One moment became a legend: Taro's token hopped home on a reckless four.

When they left, the totems had a new companion, Taro's mark. The grassland looked like a map for the first time.

Baby walked next to Borin and matched his slower steps without saying anything.

Seven cities. The moon cliffs were calling.

The seasons turned on the savannah like pages. The six helped plant millet. They fixed the totems after a storm. A child named Kesh learned Baby's name before she learned her grandmother's. It made Baby quiet for a whole day.

The dust snake came back every year. It always took a token, never a finger. Taro turned it into a game: carve, lose, carve again. "Takshak taxes the careless," he joked once. Kael did not smile. "He taxes the proud," he said. "Be careless on purpose, if you must."

The clay board cracked in the sun and was mended with copper wire. The clan loved that, like a lesson. Their champion was a woman with laugh lines and a hard endgame. Baby sat across from her for the final match, with gray showing at his temples now. She rolled, he rolled, and the crowd hummed. When his last token crossed the line, she bowed, not to him, but to the die.

The markets brought rumors: a king not yet born, a canal not yet dug, a serpent king who slept between centuries like a man between meals.

Mani bought salt and traded stories. Maya bought nothing and learned three new paths south.

Before they left, the clan painted the faces of the six on a totem. They were not gods, only travelers. Taro cried and blamed the wind.

The copper key bent once in Baby's grip during a practice roll. He straightened it with a stone and thought of the doors still unopened. The southern road stretched out pale green. The heat came back into their bones.

Kesh, the child who knew Baby's name, gave him a carved dot, one single dot, the smallest number, the first step. He wore it on a cord beside the river stone.

The rider from the Ravi Marches rode beside them for half a day, asking questions that Kael answered in circles. When they parted, the rider said, "If Sumbarta is a walk, I hope it walks south." Kael said, "It walks where the gates are."

Kael wrote: *Tamrapatha asked if we were a place. We answered like walkers. Maybe that is the only true answer.*

They stayed through a dry year, when the totems cracked and Taro mixed copper dust into clay to mend their faces. They stayed through a wet year, when the dust snake's hollow flooded and became a pond. Children sailed leaf boats on it and pretended the boats were tokens going home.

Baby taught Kesh the rule of six. It was not magic. It was discipline. Six walkers. Six sides on the die. Six steps back, where the monster had thrown Borin at Ayaskara. That number still haunted Kael's dreams.

On the last night, the clan held an all-night Royal Game in their honor. Baby did not play. He watched and let his knees rest. Mani won three games and lost two and did not care. Maya played once, won, and disappeared before anyone could congratulate her.

At dawn they took the southern track. The grass bent under their feet like a board waiting for pieces.
