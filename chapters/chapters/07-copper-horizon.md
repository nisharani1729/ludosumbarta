# Chapter 7: Copper Horizon

**Tamrapatha · Copper · The map of grass**

Tamrapatha was wind and copper and a horizon that did not end.

The village stood among totems green with weather, faces carved and repainted each spring. The clan guarded the southern way with stories more than spears. A snake lived in the dust and took Taro's token the week they arrived; he laughed, made another, and taught a child to carve the pips into soft wood.

They mined copper until their hands were the color of the totems. The work was honest and loud. Markets came on seasonal roads—a woman from the Kathua Plains traded salt; a rider from the Ravi Marches asked if Sumbarta was a place yet.

Kael said, "It is a walk that has not finished."

The rider squinted. "Walk long enough and it becomes a border."

"Or a people," Maya said.

Children of the clan learned the Royal Game as if it were speech. The six stayed long enough to see a generation of small players who had never known the reed gate. Baby felt the years then—not as numbers, as faces. Mani's hair had gray in it. Maya's had none, which Baby did not believe. Borin's knees spoke when he stood.

Taro raised a new post at the path's turning, not a god, only a mark: *the six passed here*. He carved the words shallow so rain would not erase them too quickly.

They won the gate-game on a board of baked clay. The key was copper, soft enough to bend, hard enough to open the south. The match lasted one afternoon and felt like a festival—drums, laughter, a stumble that became legend when Taro's token hopped home on a reckless four.

When they left, the totems had a new companion, Taro's mark, and the grassland looked like a map for the first time.

Baby walked beside Borin, matching his slower step without comment.

Seven cities. The moon-cliffs called.

Seasons turned on the savannah like pages. They helped plant millet. They mended totems after a storm. A child named Kesh learned Baby's name before she learned her grandmother's—an honor that made Baby quiet for a day.

The dust snake returned each year, always taking a token, never a finger. Taro made a game of it: carve, lose, carve again. "Takshak taxes the careless," he joked once. Kael did not smile. "Taxes the proud," he corrected. "Be careless on purpose if you must."

The baked-clay board cracked in the sun and was repaired with copper wire—a metaphor the clan loved. Their champion was a woman with laugh lines and ruthless endgame. Baby sat with her for the final match, his gray showing at the temples now. She rolled, he rolled, the crowd hummed. When his last token crossed, she bowed not to him but to the die.

Markets brought rumors: a king not yet born, a canal not yet dug, a serpent king who slept between centuries like a man between meals.

Mani bought salt and traded stories. Maya bought nothing and learned three new paths south.

Before departure, the clan painted their six faces on a totem—not gods, travelers. Taro cried and blamed the wind.

Copper key bent once in Baby's grip during a practice roll; he straightened it with a stone and thought of doors still unopened. The southern way stretched pale-green. Heat returned to their bones.

Kesh, the child who knew Baby's name, gave him a carved pip—one dot, the smallest number, the first step. He wore it on a cord beside the river stone.

Rider from the Ravi Marches rode alongside half a day, asking questions Kael answered in circles. At parting the rider said, "If Sumbarta is a walk, I hope it walks south." Kael said, "It walks where the gates are."

Kael wrote: *Tamrapatha asked if we were a place. We answered like walkers. Perhaps that is the only true answer.*

They stayed through a dry year when the totems cracked and Taro mixed copper dust into clay to repair faces. They stayed through a wet year when the dust snake's hollow flooded and became a pond children sailed leaf-boats on, pretending the boats were tokens heading home.

Baby taught Kesh the rule of six: not magic, discipline. Six walkers. Six sides on the die. Six paces back the monster had thrown Borin at Ayaskara—a number that still haunted Kael's dreams.

On the last night, the clan played an all-night Royal Game in their honor. Baby sat out, watching, letting his knees rest. Mani won three times and lost twice and did not care. Maya played once, won, and vanished before congratulations.

At dawn they took the southern track. Grass bent underfoot like a board waiting for pieces.
