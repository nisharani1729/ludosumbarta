# Chapter 7: Copper Horizon

**Tamrapatha · copper · the map of grass**

Tamrapatha was wind, copper, and a far line of land that never ended.

The village stood among tall carved posts. The posts were green with weather. Their faces were cut again and painted again every spring. The family guarded the southern way with stories more than spears. A snake lived in the dust. It took Taro's piece the week they arrived. He laughed, made a new one, and taught a child to cut the dots into soft wood.

They dug copper until their hands were the color of the posts. The work was honest and loud. Markets came along the roads that opened with the seasons. A woman from the Kathua Plains traded salt. A rider from the Ravi Marches asked if Sumbarta was a place yet.

Kael said, "It is a walk that has not finished."

The rider squinted. "Walk long enough, and it becomes a border."

"Or a people," said Maya.

The children learned the Royal Game like a language. The six stayed long enough to see a whole new group of small players. Those children had never known the reed gate. Baby felt the years then, not as numbers but as faces. Mani had gray in her hair. Maya had none, and Baby did not believe it. Borin's knees complained when he stood.

Taro put up a new post where the path turned. It was not a god. It was only a mark. *The six passed here.* He cut the words shallow, so the rain would not wash them away too fast.

They won the gate game on a board of baked clay. The key was copper. It was soft enough to bend, and hard enough to open the south. The match lasted one afternoon. It felt like a festival, with drums and laughter. One moment became a story people told later. Taro's piece hopped home on a reckless four.

When they left, the carved posts had a new friend. It was Taro's mark. The grassland looked like a map for the first time.

Baby walked next to Borin and matched his slower steps. He did not say anything about it.

Seven cities. The moon cliffs were calling.

The seasons turned on the open grass like pages. The six helped plant millet. They fixed the posts after a storm. A child named Kesh learned Baby's name before she learned her grandmother's. It made Baby quiet for a whole day.

The dust snake came back every year. It always took a piece, never a finger. Taro turned it into a game. Carve, lose, carve again. "Takshak taxes the careless," he joked once. Kael did not smile. "He taxes the proud," he said. "Be careless on purpose, if you must."

The clay board cracked in the sun. They mended it with copper wire. The family loved that, like a lesson. Their champion was a woman with laugh lines and a hard end of the game. Baby sat across from her for the final match. Gray showed at his temples now. She rolled. He rolled. The crowd hummed. When his last piece crossed the line, she bowed. She did not bow to him. She bowed to the die.

The markets brought rumors. A king not yet born. A canal not yet dug. A snake king who slept between centuries, like a man between meals.

Mani bought salt and traded stories. Maya bought nothing. She learned three new paths south.

Before they left, the family painted the faces of the six on a post. They were not gods. They were only travelers. Taro cried and blamed the wind.

The copper key bent once in Baby's grip during a practice roll. He straightened it with a stone and thought of the doors still closed. The southern road stretched out pale green. The heat came back into their bones.

Kesh, the child who knew Baby's name, gave him a carved dot. One single dot. The smallest number. The first step. He wore it on a cord beside the river stone.

The rider from the Ravi Marches rode beside them for half a day. He asked questions, and Kael answered in circles. When they parted, the rider said, "If Sumbarta is a walk, I hope it walks south." Kael said, "It walks where the gates are."

Kael wrote this. *Tamrapatha asked if we were a place. We answered like walkers. Maybe that is the only true answer.*

They stayed through a dry year. The posts cracked, and Taro mixed copper dust into clay to mend their faces. They stayed through a wet year. The dust snake's hollow flooded and became a pond. Children sailed leaf boats on it and pretended the boats were pieces going home.

Baby taught Kesh the rule of six. It was not magic. It was a habit of care. Six walkers. Six sides on the die. Six steps back, where the monster had thrown Borin at Ayaskara. That number still came back in Kael's dreams.

On the last night, the family held an all-night Royal Game in their honor. Baby did not play. He watched and let his knees rest. Mani won three games and lost two, and she did not care. Maya played once, won, and left before anyone could praise her.

At dawn they took the southern track. The grass bent under their feet like a board waiting for pieces.
