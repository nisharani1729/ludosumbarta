# Chapter 5: Silver Lanterns

**Rajatavana · Pale metal · A name not hers**

Rajatavana hid.

It was a village of silver lanterns in a forest, and it did not want to be found. Paths turned back on themselves. Moss swallowed footprints. The people lived as if names were a kind of smoke. Maya walked as if she had been born in that smoke. She would not say if she had.

In the roots there was a mine. It was a clay pot packed with something that flashed and threw Baby back to the last checkpoint, a tree marked with silver. There was a pit under the leaves. There was a snake that wore the lantern light like jewelry. It coiled once around a branch, and when they looked again it was gone.

Maya did not use a shield. She used knowledge. She told Kael which path was a real path and which was a trap. She spoke with the lantern-keepers in whispers. Baby followed her and learned that a leader sometimes walks behind the person who knows the dark.

In the fifth year of the silver shadows, a man of the village offered them a shorter oath: stay here, take the lantern-right, and forget the Crown.

"We could be safe," Taro said quietly. He did not say it to persuade anyone. He wanted to hear how the words felt.

Kael refused. "Safe is a house. We are a walk."

The man smiled. It was a smile like Maya's.

Two nights later, Borin found the man at their packs, holding the royal key in his fist. It was a key of pale metal, already theirs, and now stolen back. Borin did not hit him. He sat on him until morning, as heavy as a boulder, the kind the village had learned to build around.

Maya spoke to the man in a voice Baby had never heard, low and final. In the morning the man was gone, and Maya's sleeve was empty.

"What did you give him?" Baby asked.

"A name," she said. "Not mine."

They played the Royal Game in a hall with no windows. Lanterns hung from chains, and their light made the board look like a lake. The village's champion was old and slow, and cruel in small ways: an extra roll, a "forgotten" rule, a toe on the edge of the board. Kael quoted the River Oath. The old man gave way with a grunt that meant respect.

They won, and the win felt like a secret that the forest might keep or might not. When they left, Baby looked for Maya's face in every lantern. He found it, and he did not.

Taro marked a tree with a silver nail. It was his own invention, a cousin to the copper pin. Mani said they were collecting metals the way the gates collected years.

Five cities done. The peaks waited ahead, breathing gold.

In the village's last week, Baby walked the lantern paths alone. He practiced remembering turns without marking the bark, using memory to survive. He got lost once. He found his way by following the smell of fish oil, and he laughed at his own fear.

Maya taught him a trick: when a path splits, take the quieter one. "Noise is bait," she said. He asked who taught her. She said, "The smoke."

In the windowless hall, the board was inlaid with silver wire so thin it looked like frozen spider web. New rules appeared as they played. Under lantern-right, a witness could swap one roll. Under smoke-right, a witness could cancel a swap. Taro witnessed for Maya, and Mani for Baby. The game became a fight over permissions, not pieces.

Baby rolled a one when he needed a six. Maya gave him her next turn without looking at him, a kind of sisterhood among dice. At the end, Kael held three tokens in the outer ring while the champion crowded the center. It was the usual pressure play. Kael offered him a false opening and walked one quiet token home along the rim, the long road, the walker's road.

After the win, the village did not hold a feast. They dimmed the lanterns one by one, until only the board was glowing. "Secrets keep better in low light," the old champion said.

That night Baby dreamed of silver snakes. He woke and found Maya already awake, staring at the tree line.

The lanterns of Rajatavana burned fish oil with a pinch of metal dust, which made the flame whiter. After years of silver light, walking without it felt like being blind. They traveled by moonlight and habit until the mountains showed their teeth.

Maya never spoke of the man whose name she had given away. She walked at the front more often. Kael did not ask. Borin checked the packs twice at every camp, as if theft might grow back like moss.

The pale metal key hung around Kael's neck, cold against his skin. Once it chimed against a root, and everyone froze, hands on their tokens, until the silence came back.

Kael wrote: *Rajatavana taught us that theft is not always about metal. Guard the names.*
