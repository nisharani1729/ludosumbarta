# Chapter 5: Silver Lanterns

**Rajatavana · Pale metal · A name not hers**

Rajatavana hid.

Silver lanterns in a woodland hamlet that did not wish to be found. Paths turned back on themselves. Moss swallowed footprints. The clan lived as if names were a kind of smoke. Maya walked as if she had been born in that smoke. She would not say whether she had.

There was a mine in the roots, a clay pot packed with something that flashed and threw Baby back to the last stone they had called a checkpoint—a silver-marked tree. There was a pit under leaves. There was a snake that wore the lantern-light like jewelry, coiled once around a branch, gone when they looked again.

Maya used no shield. She used knowledge. She told Kael which path was a path and which was a throat. She negotiated with lantern-keepers in whispers. Baby followed and learned that leadership is sometimes walking behind someone who knows the dark.

In the fifth year of the silver shadows, a man of the hamlet offered them a shorter oath: stay, take lantern-right, forget the Crown.

"We could be safe," Taro said quietly, not to persuade—to test the words.

Kael refused. "Safe is a house. We are a walk."

The man smiled with Maya's kind of smile.

Two nights later Borin found the man at their packs with the royal key in his fist—a key of pale metal, already theirs, already stolen back. Borin did not hit him. He sat on him until morning, immovable as a boulder the hamlet had learned to build around.

Maya spoke to the man in a voice Baby had not heard, low and final. In the morning the man was gone and Maya's sleeve was empty.

"What did you give him?" Baby asked.

"A name," she said. "Not mine."

They played the Royal Game in a hall without windows. Lanterns hung from chains; their light made the board look like a lake. The hamlet's champion was old and slow and cruel in small ways—extra roll, "forgotten" rule, a toe on the rim. Kael cited the River Oath. The old one yielded with a grunt that meant respect.

They won, and the win felt like a secret the forest would keep or would not. When they left, Baby looked for Maya's face in every lantern and found it, and also did not.

Taro marked a tree with a silver nail—his own invention, a cousin to the copper pin. Mani said they were collecting metals the way the gates collected years.

Five cities. The peaks waited, breathing gold.

In the hamlet's last week, Baby walked the lantern paths alone and practiced remembering turns without marking bark—memory as survival. He got lost once, found himself by following the smell of fish oil, and laughed at his own fear.

Maya taught him a trick: when a path forks, choose the one that sounds quieter. "Noise is bait," she said. He asked who taught her. She said, "The smoke."

In the windowless hall, the board was inlaid with silver wire so fine it looked like frozen spiderwork. Rules appeared as they played—lantern-right meant a witness could swap one roll; smoke-right meant a witness could cancel a swap. Taro witnessed for Maya; Mani for Baby. The game became a duel of permissions, not pieces.

Baby rolled a one when he needed a six. Maya traded him her next turn without looking at him, a sisterhood of dice. On the final sequence Kael held three tokens in the outer ring while the champion crowded the center—classic pressure. Kael fed him a false opening and walked a quiet token home on the rim, the long road, the walker’s road.

After the win, the hamlet did not feast. They dimmed lanterns one by one until only the board glowed. "Secrets keep better in low light," the old champion said.

Baby dreamed of silver snakes that night and woke to Maya already awake, staring at the treeline.

Rajatavana's lanterns used fish oil and a pinch of metal dust that made the flame whiter. Walking without them after years of silver light felt like blindness. They traveled by moon and habit until the peaks showed their teeth.

Maya did not speak of the man she had named away. She walked point more often. Kael did not ask. Borin checked the packs twice each camp, as if theft might grow back like moss.

The pale-metal key hung on Kael's neck, cold against skin. It chimed once on a root and everyone stopped, hand on token, until silence returned.

Kael wrote: *Rajatavana taught us that theft is not always of metal. Guard the names.*
