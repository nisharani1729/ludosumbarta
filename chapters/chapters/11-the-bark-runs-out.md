# Chapter 11: The Bark Runs Out

**~900 BC · Sphatika enclosure**

Kael wrote the ending the same way he had written the beginning.

He wrote: *We were six, and then we were a kingdom. That is not the same as being six. But it is the only reason the six mattered.*

The bark was almost full. Charcoal dust stained his fingers. Smoke from the morning cook fire drifted across the crystal. It was harmless and ordinary, a small miracle after a hundred years of pits and monsters.

Baby Dufaan's hair was gray, like the old reed roofs of his childhood. He sat at the edge of the enclosure and shook the die one more time. It landed on one.

Mani laughed. It was a dry sound, but it was hers.

"Go ahead," she said. "Or don't. I am done with first steps."

Maya stood with her face in the crystal's light. For a moment she looked like a girl in a hidden village, deciding if a name was a gift or a knife. Taro put his copper needle on the board and left it there. It was a tool that had finished its work.

Baby put the die into Kael's old hand.

"Write that we were together," he said. "Write it until the bark runs out."

Kael wrote it. He wrote all six names, Borin's too, because Borin was sitting close enough to correct the spelling. He wrote about the rope. He wrote that Takshak had not been beaten, only held by rules older than his hunger. One century for each perfect walk. One board for every gate. Six who must arrive, or no game at all.

The crystal held the morning.

Somewhere far down the long road, a child in Vardhana would one day find a reed bracelet in the mud and think it was only a toy. That child had never seen them.

It was not only a toy. It was the first city noticing the grass.

And the grass, for one century, had noticed them back.

Mani put an empty pack on her shoulder. Taro took one last look at the shadow in the crystal and spat. It was not hate. It was habit. Then he turned toward the path down.

Six walkers were going home, if home still meant reeds and river, or if home had become the name they carried. Borin took the first step down and waited, as if the slope might argue with him.

Kael rolled up the bark and tied it with a cord of grass. The record would outlive his hand. That was enough.

Baby stayed one breath longer at the edge of the enclosure. In the crystal, he thought he saw a bronze scale, deep like a memory. It slid once and was still.

It was not goodbye. It was: see you in a hundred years.

He followed the others into the grass.

The canal, the palace and the neon river of futures that Kael could not imagine belonged to children not yet born. The six would walk home together. They had bought one century with ten wins, six arrivals and a wooden die worn smooth. Takshak hated that bargain, because it was fair enough to hold.

Baby looked back once. The crystal crown flashed, then hid itself in the morning.

Mani walked beside Borin for an hour without speaking. Then she said, "You wanted to take the first step down."

"I still do," said Borin.

"Six," said Baby.

"I counted," said Borin.

Taro hummed a canopy bridge song, out of tune. Maya joined in on the second line, which surprised everyone. Kael did not hum. He smiled, which was rarer.

They passed a farmer who asked where they had been. Kael said, "Naming a walk." The farmer nodded as if that were a reasonable answer in this world.

At camp, Baby shook the die one more time, alone. Six. He laughed until his eyes watered. Then he put the die away for the night.

Kael read aloud the first line and the last line of his record and compared them. The rope and the crystal. The same sentence in different weather. Mani fed the fire. Taro sharpened nothing, because nothing needed sharpening anymore.

Maya asked Kael to write one public line for the wall at Vardhana, if there ever was a wall. He thought, then wrote: *We walked so you could stand still.* She nodded. "They will hate it," she said. "Good," he said. "Hate remembers."

Baby slept and dreamed of boards without monsters, only children, only grass, only a hundred years of breathing before the next test woke up.

In the morning they would cross Vardhana's meadow again, not as founders but as witnesses to their own legend. Kael would read from the bark at the reed gate, if anyone gathered. Mani would refuse applause. Taro would fix a beam. Maya would vanish for an hour and come back with nothing to explain. Baby would kneel by the pit Mani had fallen into, now filled with rain, and leave the carved dot that Kesh had given him, a one for the start of the road.

The die would stay wooden. The oath would stay six, and they were still six.

And Takshak would count the years the way walkers count miles: patiently, hungrily, and already planning the next board. His shadow lay in the crystal, but his home was far away, in the scorched reed lands south of Sumbarta. He could wait in both places.

The bark was full. The walk went on downhill, toward reed smoke and children who would find toys in the mud and not know they were treaties.

_End._
