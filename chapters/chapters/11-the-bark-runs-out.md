# Chapter 11: The Bark Runs Out

**~900 BC · Sphatika enclosure**

Kael wrote the end the way he had written the beginning.

He wrote: *We were six, and then we were a kingdom, which is not the same as being six, and is the only reason the six mattered.*

The bark was nearly full. Char dust stained his fingers. Smoke from the morning cookfire drifted across the crystal, harmless and ordinary—a miracle after a hundred years of pits and monsters.

Baby Dufaan, whose hair was the color of the reed roofs of his childhood, sat at the enclosure's edge and shook the die once more. It came up one.

Mani laughed. It was a dry sound, but it was hers.

"Deploy," she said. "Or don't. I am done with first steps."

Maya stood with her face in the crystal's light and for a moment looked like a girl in a hidden hamlet, deciding whether a name was a gift or a knife. Taro set his copper needle on the board and left it, a tool that had finished its work.

Baby put the die in Kael's old hand.

"Write that we were together," he said. "Write it until the bark runs out."

Kael wrote it. He wrote all six names, Borin's among them, because Borin was sitting close enough to correct the spelling. He wrote the rope. He wrote that Takshak had not been beaten, only bound by rules older than his hunger—a century per perfect walk, a board for every gate, six who must arrive or no game at all.

The crystal held the morning.

Somewhere down the long walk, a child in Vardhana—a child who had never seen them—would one day find a reed bracelet in the mud and think it was only a toy.

It was not only a toy. It was the first city noticing the grass.

And the grass, for one century, had noticed them back.

Mani shouldered an empty pack. Taro took one last look at the shadow in the facet and spat—not in hatred, in habit—and turned toward the downward path.

Six walkers going home, if home still meant reed and river, or if home had become the name they carried. Borin took the first step down and waited, as if the slope might argue.

Kael rolled the bark and tied it with grass cord. The record would outlive his hand. That was enough.

Baby lingered one breath longer at the enclosure's lip. In the crystal he thought he saw a bronze scale, deep as a memory, slide once and still.

Not goodbye. See you in a hundred years.

He followed the others into the grass.

The canal and the palace and the neon river of futures Kael could not imagine belonged to children not yet born. The six would walk home together. They had bought one century with ten wins, six arrivals, and a wooden die worn smooth—a bargain Takshak hated because it was fair enough to hold.

Baby looked back once. The crystal crown flashed, then hid itself in morning.

Mani walked beside Borin without speaking for an hour, then said, "You wanted the first step down."

"I still do," Borin said.

"Six," Baby said.

"I counted," Borin said.

Taro hummed a canopy bridge-song off-key. Maya joined on the second line, surprising everyone. Kael did not hum. He smiled, which was rarer.

They passed a farmer who asked where they had been. Kael said, "Naming a walk." The farmer nodded as if that were a reasonable answer in this world.

At camp, Baby shook the die one more time in private. Six. He laughed until his eyes watered, then put the die away for the night.

Kael read aloud the first line and the last, comparing them. The rope and the crystal. The same sentence in different weather. Mani fed the fire. Taro sharpened nothing, because nothing needed sharpening anymore.

Maya asked Kael to write one public line for Vardhana's wall, if walls came. He thought, then wrote: *We walked so you could stand still.* She nodded. "They will hate it," she said. "Good," he said. "Hatred remembers."

Baby slept dreaming of boards without monsters—only children, only grass, only a century of breath before the next test woke.

In the morning they would cross Vardhana's meadow again—not as founders, as witnesses to their own legend. Kael would read from bark at the reed gate if anyone gathered. Mani would refuse applause. Taro would fix a lintel. Maya would vanish for an hour and return with nothing to explain. Baby would kneel by the pit Mani had fallen into, now filled with rain, and leave the carved pip Kesh had given him, a one for the road's beginning.

The die would stay wooden. The oath would stay six, and they were still six.

And Takshak, coiled in crystal far north, would count the years the way walkers count miles—patiently, hungrily, already planning the next board.

The bark was full. The walk continued downward, toward reed smoke and children who would find toys in mud and not know they were treaties.

_End._
