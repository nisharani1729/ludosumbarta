# Chapter 8: Moonlit Cliffs

**Chandrasaila · Moonstone · The bark record**

Chandrasaila was quiet after so much horizon.

A shepherd outpost under moonlit cliffs. Moonstone in the rock, pale and cold. The clan counted stars more than coins. Fires were small. Songs were older than the words.

There was no pit waiting, no monster in the first month. The danger was looking backward. Baby caught Kael staring toward Vardhana's direction as if he could see the rope through a hundred years of walking.

Kael began the record there in earnest, sitting with his bark and a stick of char, writing the rope and the quarry and the rain.

"If the ice takes the road from under us," he told Baby, "someone must still know we were a people becoming."

Baby sat with him on the nights Mani walked the cliffs alone. She said the height cleared her head. Sometimes she did not return until the stars had turned.

They mined moonstone with care, because the shepherds said the cliffs remembered greed. Chips were taken with prayers. One chip cut Maya's palm; she bled silver-pink in the moonlight and did not flinch.

The Royal Game was slow. The key was a white shard. Tokens clicked like teeth. The shepherd champion played for a draw until Borin forced a decision with a sacrifice Baby still did not fully understand. They won without joy and without shame, which Kael said was the correct temperature for a kingdom.

A shepherd-girl asked Baby if he had children. He said, "The road had them. I held some." It was a true answer and an empty one. She gave him milk anyway.

In the last winter of the peaks, Maya stood beside Kael's writing and said, "Leave my name out of the thefts. Put it in the wins."

Kael did both, which was his way of loving her.

Eight cities. Ice ahead. Borin's breath came harder on the climbs.

Baby counted the years without arithmetic—by how many times he had rewound the reed bracelet until it broke, by how many dies Taro had carved for teaching, by how many times the bronze snake had sent a stone backward.

Nine gates if you counted Sphatika. One life spent. One friend growing heavy in his bones.

Shepherds taught them to read weather in moonstone—clouds trapped in mineral, faint and blue. Maya could see tomorrow in a chip; she said it was guesswork and luck. Baby believed her anyway.

Mani's cliff walks became legend among the young. Once she brought back a kid goat lost on a ledge; the mother bleated her thanks. Borin could not make that climb anymore. He held the rope at the top and pretended not to mind.

The slow board at Chandrasaila punished haste. Games stretched across days. Witnesses changed shifts. Kael wrote between moves, documenting not only wins but pauses—who drank water, who slept, who stared at the moon as if negotiating with it.

In the third week of the final match, Baby offered a draw.

Shepherds brought milk and cliff-honey. Mani traded stories of iron and rain for their star-lore. Borin learned to name constellations he would never navigate, because learning was how he loved.

Moonstone chips clicked in a pouch like dice. Sometimes at night Baby shook the pouch and listened, pretending fate could be heard in mineral.

The white shard key cut Kael's thumb on the day of the win. He bled on the board and the shepherd champion said, "Good. Now it knows you." Superstition again, or religion. Hard to tell on the cliffs. The shepherd champion refused. "Kingdoms are not drawn," she said. "They are chosen." So he chose to win, one square at a time.

Maya's theft line in Kael's bark became a win line: *She bought us Rajatavana with a name. She bought us the cliffs with silence.*

They packed moonstone and char and bark enough for two more chapters, or twenty, if the ice allowed.

Ice rumors reached the cliffs before the cold did—stories of pits under snow, of sapphire that cut gloves, of a serpent black as a closed eye. Borin listened and rubbed his knees. "One more gate before the Crown," he said. "I can do one more."

Kael's bark volume grew heavy. He copied key passages onto a second strip in case the first was lost—Mani's idea, practical as a strap. Taro carved a case from cliff-wood to hold the record dry.

The shepherd-girl who gave Baby milk asked if Sumbarta would have a flag. He said, "We have a rope and a die." She looked disappointed. He added, "And ten cities that noticed us." She smiled then.

When they left Chandrasaila, moonstone chips rattled like promises. The cliffs watched them go without judgment.
