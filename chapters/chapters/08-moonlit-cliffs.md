# Chapter 8: Moonlit Cliffs

**Chandrasaila · Moonstone · The bark record**

Chandrasaila was quiet after the wide horizon.

It was a shepherd camp under cliffs lit by the moon. The rock held moonstone, pale and cold. The clan counted stars more than coins. Their fires were small. Their songs were older than the words.

There was no pit waiting here, and no monster in the first month. The danger was looking back. Baby saw Kael staring toward Vardhana, as if he could see the rope across a hundred years of walking.

Kael began his record in earnest there. He sat with his bark and a stick of charcoal and wrote about the rope, the quarry and the rain.

"If the ice takes the road from under us," he told Baby, "someone must still know that we were a people becoming."

Baby sat with him on the nights when Mani walked the cliffs alone. She said the height cleared her head. Sometimes she did not come back until the stars had turned.

They mined moonstone carefully, because the shepherds said the cliffs remembered greed. Every chip was taken with a prayer. One chip cut Maya's palm. She bled silver-pink in the moonlight and did not flinch.

The Royal Game was slow. The key was a white shard. The tokens clicked like teeth. The shepherd champion played for a draw, until Borin forced a result with a sacrifice that Baby still did not fully understand. They won without joy and without shame. Kael said that was the right temperature for a kingdom.

A shepherd girl asked Baby if he had children. He said, "The road had them. I held some." It was a true answer, and an empty one. She gave him milk anyway.

In the last winter in the mountains, Maya stood beside Kael while he wrote. "Leave my name out of the thefts," she said. "Put it in the wins."

Kael did both. That was his way of loving her.

Eight cities. Ice ahead. Borin's breath came harder on the climbs.

Baby counted the years without arithmetic. He counted by the times he had wound the reed bracelet back together until it broke, by the dice Taro had carved for teaching, and by the times a snake had taken a token from him.

Two gates left, Nilahima and then Sphatika. One life spent. One friend getting heavy in his bones.

The shepherds taught them to read weather in moonstone, in the faint blue clouds trapped inside the mineral. Maya could see tomorrow in a chip. She said it was only guessing and luck. Baby believed her anyway.

Mani's cliff walks became a legend among the young. Once she brought back a kid goat that was stuck on a ledge, and its mother bleated her thanks. Borin could no longer make that climb. He held the rope at the top and pretended he did not mind.

The slow board at Chandrasaila punished hurry. Games lasted for days. Witnesses took turns. Kael wrote between moves. He recorded not only the wins but the pauses: who drank water, who slept, who stared at the moon as if bargaining with it.

In the third week of the final match, Baby offered a draw.

The shepherd champion refused. "Kingdoms are not drawn," she said. "They are chosen." So Baby chose to win, one square at a time.

The shepherds brought milk and cliff honey. Mani traded stories of iron and rain for their star lore. Borin learned the names of constellations he would never travel under. Learning was how he showed love.

Moonstone chips clicked in a pouch like dice. Sometimes at night, Baby shook the pouch and listened, pretending he could hear fate in the stone.

On the day of the win, the white shard key cut Kael's thumb. He bled on the board. The shepherd champion said, "Good. Now it knows you." It was superstition, or maybe religion. On the cliffs it was hard to tell.

In Kael's bark, the line about Maya's thefts became a line about her wins: *She bought us Rajatavana with a name. She bought us the cliffs with silence.*

They packed moonstone, charcoal and enough bark for two more chapters, or twenty, if the ice allowed.

Rumors of ice reached the cliffs before the cold did. People told stories of pits under the snow, of sapphire that cut through gloves, and of a serpent as black as a closed eye. Borin listened and rubbed his knees. "One more gate before the Crown," he said. "I can do one more."

Kael's bark was getting heavy. He copied the most important parts onto a second strip, in case the first one was lost. That was Mani's idea, as practical as a strap. Taro carved a case out of cliff wood to keep the record dry.

The shepherd girl who had given Baby milk asked if Sumbarta would have a flag. He said, "We have a rope and a die." She looked disappointed. So he added, "And ten cities that noticed us." Then she smiled.

When they left Chandrasaila, the moonstone chips rattled like promises. The cliffs watched them go without judging them.
