# Chapter 2: Iron Dune Quarry

**Ayaskara · Black iron · The rust monster**

Ayaskara was not a town. It was a caravan camp around a wound in the dunes, where men pulled iron out of the earth as if the earth were a hide. The clan called it Iron Dune Quarry. Smoke rose day and night. The air tasted of filings. There was no snake in the grass. There was something worse.

They arrived in a year when the foreman had lost three men to a collapse. He looked at the six as if they were either salvation or extra mouths.

"We don't play games for strangers," he said.

Kael showed the reed bracelet on Baby's wrist, faded but still tied. The foreman's eyes narrowed.

"The meadow sent you. The dunes will test you."

The test came at dusk on a wind that tasted of rust. Baby would never find a better word for what followed. It was the quarry's own hunger given shoulders—a shape without a face that moved like a man and hit like a landslide. It drove Borin six paces back down the path they had climbed, and then six more, as if the road itself had spat him out. Maya's shield, unused since Vardhana, broke on it with a sound like a wet branch. The monster took the break as payment and was gone, leaving only iron dust swirling and a silence that felt like Takshak laughing without a mouth.

Borin sat in the dust and laughed once, which frightened Baby more than screaming would have.

"I thought I was the shield," he said.

Kael did not mine here. The camp already bled metal. Instead they worked the foreman's tally—ore weighed, slag sorted—until he owed them the right to search. They recovered a key of black iron from a box under the foreman's stone, cold enough to burn the tongue.

The Royal Game was scratched into a wagon floor with nail and charcoal. Quarry men played to wound: elbows on the rim, sharp jokes, tokens slammed. Kael played to end. He sacrificed his lead twice so Mani could enter the home row. On the final roll Taro held his breath so hard the copper pin left a mark on his lip. Borin blocked the foreman's last advance with a move so plain it looked like stupidity until it wasn't.

When they won, the foreman spat and then poured them water, which in Ayaskara was the same as a blessing.

They stayed long enough for Taro to learn the bellows and for Baby to stop dreaming of the pit every night—though he dreamed of the rust shape longer. Mani learned the dunes: which slopes sang before they slid, where mirage water lied. Maya learned who stole whose ore and spoke to a thief in a language Baby did not know; the thief returned a pouch the next day and would not meet Maya's eyes.

In the last year a boy from the camp asked to walk with them. He had clever hands and a scar across his knuckles.

Kael said no.

"The oath is six," he said. "If we become an army, we become a different story."

The boy watched them leave. Baby looked back until the camp was only a stain on the iron.

On the dune's lip, Kael wrote on bark for the first time—a single line: *The road can throw you back six and six. Count anyway.*

Mani took the first step down the far slope.

The boy from the camp brought them flatbread on the last morning—no words, only food. Baby left his second-best scoop for him, gourd and stick, the kind of gift that means *I see you* without breaking the oath.

At night, before the dunes, they practiced the Royal Game on packed sand until the stars wheeled. Mani accused Kael of counting too loudly. Taro carved temporary tokens from slag. Maya won three times and refused to boast.

Kael dreamed of the rust shape and woke with the word *Takshak* on his tongue. He did not speak it aloud. The dunes were listening.

On the wagon-board, Baby learned that quarry rules allowed a player to forfeit a turn to "cool the iron"—a superstition that became strategy when Kael used two forfeits in a row to force the foreman's champion to overcommit. Mani knocked her knee against the table once, accidentally on purpose, rattling the die. The foreman glared. Maya smiled like innocence.

When victory came, the camp's women brought sour tea that stripped the rust taste from their mouths. An old digger touched Baby's forehead with a blackened thumb. "You walked in hungry country," he said. "Walk out before it walks into you."

They traded a wedge of amber for dried meat and iron nails. Taro pocketed nails as if they were dice. Everything was a tool if you loved it enough.

Two cities behind them. Eight ahead.

Baby counted pips on the wagon-board until he could dream them. Mani counted steps. Taro counted nails. Maya counted faces that owed favors. Borin counted nothing aloud; his silence was its own arithmetic.

At the dune's edge, the foreman shouted after them: "If the serpent king comes, tell him the quarry paid in blood already!" Kael raised a hand—acknowledgment, not agreement—and did not look back.
