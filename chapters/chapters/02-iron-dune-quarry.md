# Chapter 2: Iron Dune Quarry

**Ayaskara · black iron · the rust monster**

Ayaskara was not a real town. It was a camp for travelers, built around a wound in the sand hills. Men dug iron out of the earth as if the earth were an animal's skin. Smoke rose day and night. The air tasted of metal dust. There were no snakes in the grass here. There was something worse.

They arrived in a year when the work boss had lost three men in a cave-in. He looked at the six as if they were either his rescue or just more mouths to feed.

"We don't play games for strangers," he said.

Kael showed him the reed bracelet on Baby's wrist. It had faded, but it was still tied.

The boss narrowed his eyes. "The meadow sent you. The dunes will test you."

The test came at dusk, on a wind that tasted of rust. Baby never found a better word for what came next. It was the hunger of the quarry, and it had a body. It had no face. It moved like a man and hit like a landslide. It threw Borin six steps back down the path they had climbed, and then six more. It was as if the road itself had spat him out. Maya's shield, unused since Vardhana, broke against it. The sound was like a wet branch. The monster took the broken shield as payment and vanished. Only iron dust was left, turning in the air. The silence felt like Takshak laughing without a mouth.

Borin sat in the dust and laughed once. That frightened Baby more than a scream would have.

"I thought I was the shield," he said.

Kael did not dig here. The camp was already bleeding metal. Instead, they did the boss's work. They weighed the ore and sorted the waste, until he owed them the right to search. Under the boss's stone, they found a key of black iron. It was so cold it burned the tongue.

The Royal Game was scratched on a wagon floor with a nail and charcoal. The quarry men played to hurt. They leaned on the edge, made sharp jokes, and slammed their pieces down. Kael played to finish the game. Twice he gave up his lead so Mani could enter the home row. On the last roll, Taro held his breath so hard that the copper pin left a mark on his lip. Borin blocked the boss's last move with a play so simple it looked foolish, until it was not.

When the six won, the boss spat on the ground and then poured them water. In Ayaskara, that was the same as a blessing.

They stayed long enough for Taro to learn the bellows. Baby stopped dreaming of the pit every night. He dreamed of the rust shape for much longer. Mani learned the dunes. She learned which slopes sang before they slid, and where the fake water lied. Maya learned who stole whose ore. She spoke to one thief in a language Baby did not know. The next day the thief returned a stolen bag and would not look at her.

In the last year, a boy from the camp asked to walk with them. He had clever hands and a scar across his knuckles.

Kael said no.

"The promise says six," he said. "If we become an army, we become a different story."

The boy watched them leave. Baby kept looking back until the camp was only a stain on the iron.

At the top of the dune, Kael wrote on the bark for the first time. It was one line. *The road can throw you back six steps, and six more. Count anyway.*

Mani took the first step down the far slope.

On the last morning, the boy brought them flat bread. He said nothing. Baby left him his second-best scoop, made of a gourd and a stick. The gift meant "I see you" without breaking the promise.

The night before, they had practiced the Royal Game on packed sand until the stars turned. Mani said Kael counted too loudly. Taro cut temporary pieces from waste rock. Maya won three times and would not boast.

Kael dreamed of the rust shape and woke with the word *Takshak* on his tongue. He did not say it. The dunes were listening.

On the wagon board, Baby learned a quarry rule. A player could skip a turn "to cool the iron." It was an old belief, but it became a plan when Kael skipped twice in a row. That made the boss's champion rush and make mistakes. Mani bumped the table with her knee, "by accident," and shook the die. The boss glared. Maya smiled like an innocent child.

After the win, the women of the camp brought a sour tea. It washed the rust taste from their mouths. An old digger touched Baby's forehead with a black thumb. "You walked through hungry country," he said. "Leave before it walks into you."

They traded a piece of amber for dried meat and iron nails. Taro put the nails in his pocket as if they were dice. He believed anything could be a tool if you loved it enough.

Two cities behind them. Eight ahead.

Baby counted dots on the wagon board until he could see them in his dreams. Mani counted steps. Taro counted nails. Maya counted the people who owed her favors. Borin counted nothing out loud. His silence was its own kind of counting.

At the edge of the dunes, the boss shouted after them. "If the snake king comes, tell him the quarry has already paid in blood!"

Kael raised a hand. It meant he had heard. It did not mean he agreed. He did not look back.
